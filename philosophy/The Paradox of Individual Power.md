## Part 1: The Circle of Power

An individual in society is held accountable by their peers. Those peers are themselves made up of individuals, who hold each other accountable within the group.

But in a society, the bias of those individuals can override the will of any one individual. So a higher order of individuals is required to create and enforce the safety of the singular individual, an institution of sorts.

The problem is that the institution itself is composed of individuals. This creates a circular power system, where the individuals of the institution can be influenced by power, which in turn affects the singular individual they are meant to protect.

Likewise, said individual within the institution can be held accountable by others within the institution, but this assumes the collective group cannot come to similar conclusions, which would only create a greater concentration of power. This concentration of power now grants the individual in the institution, and in effect, the group, to enforce their will on those who rely on them. In this case, the individual in society.

This is a circular system, where those at the end of the line control those at the start.

## Part 2: The Optics Problem

Anything the *lower* individual, and in effect their peers, try to enforce as accountability can, in effect, become an optics show. Because those in power can always create the illusion of free will.&#32;

Accountability requires an individual or a group to provide the nessesary information and at times, report their own failure. This is a crux in any high-trust society. Those at the top have access to the white paper of the novel, while those below live inside the novel.&#32;

Humanity has tried to counter this by spreading power across sectors, roles and creating a web of accountability between those given high trust roles. However, no matter how strong the web, it will always have holes. Likewise, the assumption of trust given to each participant of the web relies on the false certainty of good will. ## Part 3: The Nature of Power

As shown by George Orwell in Animal Farm, power is not necessarily derived from greed. In fact, power is a natural consequence of many factors, from wisdom, to the ability to protect, to intelligence. Many begin with good faith, but the nature of any intelligent species, as the book implied, is that power can create the very vacuum the individual tried to escape. Napoleon and Snowball had different visions for a better life, yet at the same time, power was the natural consequence of their dreams.

Which leads back to the original point: the individual has power, the collective has more power. No matter how horizontal it scales, power always goes up. No matter how vertical it scales, power will always drive itself up, because eventually someone will be driven toward individual power.
